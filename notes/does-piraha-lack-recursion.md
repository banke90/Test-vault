# Does Pirahã lack recursion, and if so, is universal grammar in trouble?

> Daniel Everett's 2005 claim that the Amazonian language Pirahã has no recursion — no clauses embedded inside clauses — would, if true, falsify the 2002 Hauser-Chomsky-Fitch proposal that recursion is the sole universal feature of human language; two decades of back-and-forth has produced a clear methodological dispute, real ethnographic scarcity, and no consensus.

## TL;DR

Daniel Everett's 2005 *Current Anthropology* paper, drawing on decades of fieldwork on the Amazonian language Pirahã, argued the language lacks embedded clauses — which would falsify [Hauser, Chomsky & Fitch 2002]'s proposal that recursion is *the* universal narrow-language faculty. **Nevins, Pesetsky and Rodrigues 2009** countered that Everett had misread the grammar and that Pirahã has recursion via nominalisation; Everett and Sakel 2009 pushed back. As of 2026 the empirical dispute is unresolved — access is limited, the corpus is modest, both sides publish, reasonable linguists disagree. Even if it were settled, what it implies for universal grammar depends on whether "recursion" means what the 2002 paper meant by it.

## What Pirahã is

- **Pirahã** (pronounced roughly *pee-da-HAN*) is spoken by the Pirahã people of the Maici River basin in Amazonas, Brazil.
- Speakers: ~400 (Everett, most recent figures).
- Classification: language isolate within the Mura family (the only surviving Mura language).
- Phonology: unusually small — 10 or 11 phonemes depending on count. Two tones.
- The language is whistled and hummed as naturally as spoken. The whistled register, in particular, preserves most of the grammar.

## Everett's claims

Everett's 2005 *Current Anthropology* paper [Everett 2005], expanded in his 2008 book *Don't Sleep, There Are Snakes*, argued that Pirahã lacks:

- **Numerals.** No words for exact quantities. Terms that appear number-like (*hói*, *hoí*) mean something like "small quantity" and "larger quantity" and shift contextually. This claim is supported by experimental cognitive work by [Gordon 2004] showing that Pirahã adults fail exact-number matching tasks above ~3 items.
- **Colour terms**, except as descriptive phrases ("blood-like," "bird-egg-like").
- **Perfect tense** — no grammatical way to express "have done."
- **Creation myths** or an elaborated cosmology.
- **Embedding / recursion.** No subordinate clauses. What look like embedded structures in surface English translations are, Everett argues, actually coordinated clauses in Pirahã.

Everett's overall hypothesis is the **Immediacy of Experience Principle (IEP)**: Pirahã culture forbids discussion of things not directly witnessed by the speaker, or by someone the speaker is talking to who witnessed them. This cultural principle, Everett argues, shapes the grammar — inhibiting abstraction, numeric quantification, and recursive embedding, because all of these are routes to referring to things beyond immediate experience.

## Why recursion specifically matters

In the 1950s–1980s, generative grammar (Chomsky and his intellectual descendants) proposed **Universal Grammar (UG)** — an innate, species-specific cognitive architecture supporting human language. Over time, what was considered "universal" narrowed.

[Hauser, Chomsky & Fitch 2002], in *Science*, narrowed it to a single operation: **Merge** (roughly, recursive combination of elements into hierarchical structures). They called this the **Faculty of Language in the Narrow Sense (FLN)** and proposed it might be *the* genuinely unique human cognitive adaptation for language — everything else (speech production, semantic categorisation, etc.) being shared with other cognitive domains or other species.

If FLN is recursion-via-Merge, then a language without recursion would falsify the theory. Hence Everett 2005's explosive effect: a working natural human language, if he was right, used no recursion, and therefore Merge cannot be what it was claimed to be.

## The technical dispute

**Nevins, Pesetsky, Rodrigues 2009** [Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues 2009] in *Language* argued that Everett's empirical description was wrong. Specifically:

- What Everett treats as **coordinated** clauses in Pirahã are, on their analysis, **embedded** clauses marked by a specific nominalising suffix (-*sai*).
- Pirahã does have clauses that contain clauses — it's just that the marking is done by a different grammatical device than English's finite complementisers.
- Several of Everett's specific examples can be reanalysed as containing embedded structure.

**Everett's response (2009)** [Everett 2009] in the same journal:
- The proposed "embedded" analyses do not fit the actual distribution of sentences in his corpus.
- The NPR critique relies on Everett's own earlier (1986) transcriptions, which Everett says were made under a theoretical framework he has since abandoned.
- Independent fieldwork is needed; until then, the data favour his current analysis.

**Sakel 2012** and others have argued that the Nevins et al. re-analysis does not hold up on linguistic grounds — i.e. siding with Everett. **Futrell, Stearns, Piantadosi, Everett & Gibson 2016** [Futrell et al. 2016] provided a corpus-based quantitative study of Pirahã grammar that supports (but does not conclusively prove) the absence of embedded structures as Everett characterises them.

My honest reading of the dispute as a non-specialist: both sides are serious; the data are thin enough that both can be defended; the fieldwork bottleneck is real. *(contested, with the dispute centred on interpretation of a small corpus more than on observable phenomena)*

## Even if Pirahã lacks recursion — does UG fail?

A separate question. Several responses have been proposed by generativists even granting the Everett position:

1. **Recursion as *capacity*, not use.** Perhaps Pirahã speakers *can* produce embedded structures but their grammar conventionally doesn't. Just as English speakers don't use five-clause-deep centre embeddings in practice, a language could under-use recursion without lacking the capacity. But this reduces the empirical content of "universal recursion" to something closer to "universal potential recursion," which is harder to falsify.

2. **FLN minimalism.** Maybe recursion is a cognitive capacity of the human mind, not a feature of every language. A language being an "edge case" of that capacity would not falsify the cognitive claim, only a strong linguistic version of it.

3. **UG without recursion.** Some generativists have retreated from the 2002 HCF position to more conservative claims about UG — that there is *some* innate language-specific architecture, but recursion may not be the unique component. This is less an ad-hoc defence than a widely held post-2010 position.

4. **Everett-plus-universal-grammar compatibility.** Pirahã might be unusual but still realise UG principles in an unusual way — specifically, using prosodic or morphological marking instead of syntactic embedding.

The honest summary: the 2002 HCF claim is at least in *empirical trouble*, independent of whether Everett's specific Pirahã description is right. Universal Grammar as a research programme continues but without the 2002 version's clean central thesis *(mixed — the empirical question and the theoretical implication are partly decoupled)*.

## The cultural and ethnographic dimension

Everett's IEP hypothesis — that Pirahã culture shapes Pirahã grammar — is orthogonal to the recursion question and more directly at odds with strong nativist linguistics. If culture can shape grammatical features this deeply, the line between cultural and biological determinants of language shifts. This aspect of Everett's work has drawn sympathy from Whorfians (cultural relativity) and hostility from nativists; the empirical traction remains modest.

Everett himself has increasingly framed his position in the broader context of "language as a cultural tool" (Everett 2012 book title). This frames Pirahã as an extreme but legitimate point in a space of possible human languages, not as an error in linguistic theory.

## Disagreements and cautions

- **Data access is thin.** Almost all detailed Pirahã data come from Everett or his collaborators. Independent fieldwork (e.g. Jeanette Sakel) exists but is limited. Any empirical claim rests on a small corpus.
- **Polemic has occasionally outrun evidence on both sides.** Everett's popular book and Chomsky's public dismissals have both contributed more heat than light. Read the primary linguistics.
- **"Pirahã lacks numbers"** is sometimes popularly extended to "Pirahã cannot think numerically." That's not what the Gordon 2004 results show; speakers can perform small-quantity matching, just not exact-match tasks above ~3.
- **IEP as an explanation** is ambitious and, in my reading, the weakest link in Everett's case. It is hard to test that culture *causes* the grammar rather than coexists with it.
- **"Recursion" itself is ambiguous.** Mathematicians, linguists, and popular writers use the term slightly differently. The Hauser-Chomsky-Fitch proposal uses it in a specific technical sense (Merge-style hierarchical combination) that does not fully map to the intuitive "a story inside a story" sense. Debates sometimes talk past each other on this.
- **The Nevins et al. re-analysis** is a serious piece of generative-linguistic work, not just a defensive reaction. Readers should not dismiss it.

## Questions I'd like answered

1. **Would independent long-term fieldwork by non-Everett-affiliated linguists** resolve the descriptive question about Pirahã embedding? It would take years and community buy-in, but is in principle achievable.
2. **Is there another language** — outside the Pirahã controversy — that cleanly lacks recursion? If Pirahã is the only candidate after decades of typological survey, that's either evidence the case is real and rare, or evidence the empirical claim doesn't survive scrutiny in general.
3. **What does Pirahã look like when recorded by speakers themselves**, in digital form, over the next decade? The language is endangered; digital preservation is proceeding (e.g. the ELDP archive). Does the expanded corpus support Everett or Nevins?
4. **Can modern LLMs help?** Large transformer models can parse Pirahã sentences; whether their preferred parse structures align with Everett's coordinate analysis or Nevins' embedded analysis is a curious, if imperfect, outside probe.
5. **Independent of the Pirahã case**, is there a weaker universal-grammar claim that survives both the Pirahã data and the broader typological record? This is where generative linguistics has largely gone.
