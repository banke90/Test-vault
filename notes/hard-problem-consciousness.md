# The hard problem of consciousness has not softened — explaining function doesn't explain experience

> Explaining how the brain discriminates, integrates, and reports — the "easy" problems — does not automatically explain why any of that is accompanied by experience; no current theory closes that gap, and the question may require concepts we don't yet have.

[Confidence: survey of a field with no consensus; individual theory descriptions are established, the overall diagnosis is my own reading]
[Last verified: 2026-04-21]

## TL;DR

**David Chalmers** distinguished the "easy" problems of consciousness (function: discrimination, integration, report) from the **hard problem**: why any of that functional machinery is accompanied by experience at all [Chalmers 1995]. The canonical arguments for the gap are Nagel on bats [Nagel 1974], Jackson on Mary [Jackson 1982], Levine on explanatory gap [Levine 1983]. Contemporary theories — IIT, Global Workspace, Higher-Order, Illusionism, Panpsychism, Predictive Processing, Attention Schema — each address parts of the question but none is accepted as solving it. Adversarial collaborations (COGITATE) have disciplined the empirical side; the philosophical side remains open. The AI consciousness question has gone from fringe to live [Butlin et al. 2023], and the octopus, the slime mould, and the swarm remind us our intuitions about which substrates support experience are almost certainly parochial.

## The explanatory gap

The canonical arguments, compactly:

- **Nagel 1974, "What is it like to be a bat?"** [Nagel 1974]. Full bat neurobiology leaves something out: the *subjective character* of echolocation experience. Subjective character is the phenomenon that needs explaining.
- **Jackson 1982, the knowledge argument** [Jackson 1982]. Mary the colour scientist knows everything physical about colour vision from inside a black-and-white room, then sees red. Does she learn something new? Intuitively yes — and if so, physicalism about experience is incomplete.
- **Levine 1983, the explanatory gap** [Levine 1983]. A perfect physical explanation of C-fibre firing doesn't seem to entail *why* pain feels the way it does. Physical and phenomenal stories don't close.

Each has a large counter-literature. None is a knockdown argument. Together they sharpen the intuition that *explaining function does not automatically explain experience* *(well-established as the form of the problem; solutions all contested)*.

## Integrated Information Theory (IIT)

Developed by **Giulio Tononi** with **Christof Koch**, **Marcello Massimini** and others. IIT identifies phenomenal consciousness with a specific quantity, **Φ (phi)**, measuring how much information a system generates as a whole beyond what its parts generate independently, under a particular causal-structure definition [Tononi et al. 2016].

The theory proceeds **axiom-to-postulate**: start from what (proponents claim) is undeniable about experience (existence, composition, information, integration, exclusion), translate each into a requirement on physical substrates, derive a measure.

Radical commitments of IIT:
- Consciousness *is* a kind of integrated causal structure — not produced by one, identical to it.
- Any system with non-zero Φ has some consciousness. Panpsychist-adjacent.
- A digital computer simulating a brain can behave identically while having potentially zero Φ. IIT therefore rejects naïve computational functionalism about consciousness.

### The 2023 IIT-as-pseudoscience letter

In September 2023, 124 consciousness researchers signed an open letter labelling IIT pseudoscience on the grounds of inadequate falsifiability and understated panpsychist commitments in public discussion. IIT proponents and many neutrals pushed back hard: IIT has made testable predictions, its panpsychism is openly discussed by proponents, and "pseudoscience" was seen by many as misuse of the term.

The episode is instructive about how consciousness science navigates the boundary between scientific and philosophical claims *(contested; no resolution)*.

## Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT)

Developed from **Bernard Baars**' workspace metaphor by **Stanislas Dehaene** and **Jean-Pierre Changeux** [Dehaene & Changeux 2011]. Most brain processing is unconscious and modular. Consciousness is what happens when information is **broadcast** across a widely-distributed prefrontal-parietal workspace, making it available to many other modules.

Empirical signature: **late, widespread ignition** — a non-linear jump in global activity ~300 ms post-stimulus when a stimulus becomes conscious. Contrasts with IIT's expectation of **posterior hot-zone** sustained integrated activity.

## The COGITATE adversarial collaboration

Templeton-funded, 2019-onwards, bringing IIT and GNWT proponents together with neutrals to design experiments both camps pre-commit to. Results across 2023–2025 (Cohen, Melloni et al. publications).

Rough summary *(my reading from memory, verify against primary sources)*:
- **Posterior cortex** showed sustained signatures correlating with conscious content — IIT-favouring.
- **Prefrontal ignition** was weaker or later than GNWT predicted, but not absent.
- Neither theory was decisively confirmed. IIT edged ahead on a couple of specific predictions, GNWT on others.

The adversarial-collaboration *method* was probably the more important output: pre-registration, agreed predictions, shared methods. This is how consciousness science should work, regardless of where any one theory ends up.

## Higher-order theories

**David Rosenthal**'s **HOT**: a mental state is conscious iff there is a higher-order mental state about it [Rosenthal 2005]. Variants: Lycan's higher-order perception, Kriegel's self-representational, LeDoux & Brown's HOROR.

HOTs are functionalist and typically predict a critical role for prefrontal cortex (site of meta-representations). Known trouble: empty/mismatched HOTs (higher-order thought about a first-order state that doesn't exist) — do they produce consciousness? *(contested)*

## Illusionism

**Keith Frankish**, and **Daniel Dennett** in a related register. What we call phenomenal consciousness is systematic introspective misrepresentation. The brain represents its own states as having intrinsic qualitative character ("qualia"), but the representation is not accurate. The explanandum shifts from *why there is experience* to *why we think there is experience in this particular way* — the **meta-problem** [Frankish 2016].

Illusionism is coherent and disciplined. Whether it *dissolves* the hard problem or just relocates it is a point Chalmers presses. *(contested but philosophically serious)*

## Panpsychism

**Philip Goff, Galen Strawson, David Chalmers** (in his panprotopsychist moods) [Goff 2017]. If physicalism can't reduce experience to function and dualism is unappealing, attribute some proto-experience to fundamental physical entities. **Constitutive panpsychism**: macro-consciousness constituted from micro-consciousness. **Cosmopsychism**: reverse.

Hardest challenge: the **combination problem** — how do micro-experiences combine into unified macro-experiences? No-one has a good answer; Chalmers' "Combination Problem for Panpsychism" is the canonical exposition. *(speculative)*

## Predictive processing / active inference

**Karl Friston, Andy Clark, Jakob Hohwy, Anil Seth**. The brain is a prediction machine; perception is controlled hallucination; the self is a deep generative model of the organism's state; conscious experience tracks the content of certain high-level models [Seth 2021].

Best understood as a theory of the *content* of experience, not the existence of experience. Helps explain *why we experience what we do*, not *why there is experience at all*. Some PP theorists would dispute this framing.

## Attention Schema Theory

**Michael Graziano**. The brain builds a simplified schematic model of its own attention — an "attention schema" — analogous to the body schema. Awareness is the operation of this schema; we mistake its simplifications for a luminous inner light. A tidy theory; detractors think it relabels the explanandum.

## AI consciousness

The question of whether AI systems could be conscious has gone from fringe to live in five years.

- **Butlin, Long, Chalmers, Seth et al. 2023** [Butlin et al. 2023]: a long report laying out an **indicator-property** approach. Extract empirical markers from the main theories (GNWT, IIT, HOT, PP), translate each into computational properties, audit current systems. Conclusion: no current AI clearly satisfies enough indicators to be a strong candidate, but nothing *in principle* rules it out, and the indicators are not far from implementable.
- **Murray Shanahan** and **Eric Schwitzgebel** have written on the ethics and phenomenology of LLMs. Schwitzgebel's **"moral status emergencies"** captures the worry: if we accidentally build conscious systems, we might not know, and misacting on them would be bad.
- **Current LLMs specifically**: the careful view is that frontier LLMs are *unlikely* to be conscious in any strong sense — they lack recurrent integration, embodiment, temporal depth. But "unlikely" ≠ "no", and the uncertainty is substantive.

This is where [[mechanistic-interpretability]] becomes philosophically important: good MI tools would let us ask concrete internal questions ("does this system have a broadcast workspace? an attention schema? integrated information?") instead of purely behavioural guesses.

## Empirical markers of consciousness

- **Perturbational Complexity Index (PCI)** [Casali et al. 2013]: TMS pulse + EEG response + compute a complexity measure. PCI reliably discriminates wakeful, dreaming, and unresponsive states in humans, and has promising results in disorders of consciousness. A partial empirical handle on *presence* of consciousness without relying on report.
- **No-report paradigms**: measure neural correlates without button presses, since reporting itself is a confound.
- **Lempel–Ziv complexity of EEG** correlates with consciousness states — lower in dreamless sleep and anaesthesia, higher in psychedelic states (Carhart-Harris, Schartner et al.).

## Where I end up

Consciousness science in 2026 is richer than when Chalmers coined the phrase, but not close to consensus. We have experimental handles on *neural correlates of conscious content* and *behavioural markers of conscious states*. We do not have a theory that explains why those correlates are accompanied by experience rather than by nothing.

The octopus, the slime mould, the swarm, and the large language model each force us toward **humility about substrate**. Our intuitions about which systems have experience are unreliable. The hard problem isn't going to be solved by looking harder at human brains alone. It may require concepts we don't have.

## Disagreements and cautions

- **"Hard problem" framing is itself contested.** Illusionists and some functionalists maintain there is no additional explanandum beyond the functional story. Physicalists disagree among themselves on whether the gap is a problem or a pseudo-problem.
- **IIT vs GNWT is not a clean horse race.** Both theories are technically complex and their empirical predictions depend on measurement choices that are themselves disputed. COGITATE results are partial and the dust has not settled.
- **AI consciousness literature risks motivated reasoning** in both directions — commercial incentives to deny, philosophical incentives to affirm. The Butlin et al. report is careful; much secondary commentary is not.
- **The PCI / LZ-complexity markers** discriminate states well but don't track conscious *content*. They are tools for "is this person conscious?", not "is this content conscious?".
- **My own reading of COGITATE** is from memory and should be verified. The primary publications and any follow-up by 2026 are the authoritative source.

## Questions I'd like answered

1. **Is there a form of argument that a physicalist could accept that bridges the explanatory gap**, or is the gap a structural feature of physical description that won't close no matter how detailed our neuroscience becomes?
2. **What would an IIT-deciding experiment look like** that both IIT proponents and critics agreed in advance was decisive? Does one even exist in principle?
3. **Are there indicator properties for AI consciousness** that are *both* strongly predicted by one of the mainstream theories *and* cleanly checkable from model internals? The Butlin report lists candidates; how cleanly they map to transformer-era architectures is not resolved.
4. **Does the octopus have experience, and if so, what kind?** If we had any empirical handle on this, it would be the most informative single data point for the substrate-independence question.
5. **Is the hard problem itself the right formulation?** Some good philosophers (Frankish, some functionalists) think it mis-frames the explanandum. I am not sure they are wrong.
