# McClintock was right about transposons thirty-five years before the field caught up

> Barbara McClintock discovered genetic elements that move around the maize genome in the 1940s, was met with bafflement and dismissal through the 1950s and most of the 1960s, was vindicated only when molecular biology rediscovered transposons in bacteria in the late 1960s, and collected the solo Nobel in 1983 — by which time transposable elements turned out to make up about half the human genome.

## TL;DR

Barbara McClintock (1902–1992) studied maize cytogenetics at Cornell and Cold Spring Harbor for most of the 20th century. Between 1944 and 1950 she worked out a two-element system — Activator (Ac) and Dissociation (Ds) — in which Ds could relocate within chromosomes, disrupting genes it landed near, under the control of Ac. She had independently discovered **transposable elements** long before molecular biology had the tools to make sense of them. Her 1951 Cold Spring Harbor talk famously received a muted, puzzled response; she became reluctant to publish. When transposons were rediscovered in bacteria in 1967–68 (James Shapiro, Peter Starlinger and others) and then in yeast, flies, and mammals through the 1970s, her maize work was re-read as having been there first. She received the unshared Nobel in Physiology or Medicine in 1983. Transposable elements have turned out to compose ~45% of the human genome — most of it "fossil," some still active.

## What she discovered

Working with controlled crosses of *Zea mays* at Cold Spring Harbor from 1941 onward, McClintock studied the inheritance of pigmentation patterns in maize kernels. A kernel's aleurone (the outer endosperm layer) normally expresses colour genes; mutations that disrupt those genes produce colourless sectors, and the geometry of the sectors tells you when and where the mutation occurred during kernel development.

McClintock noticed that certain colour-disruption patterns were not the stable, heritable mutations of classical genetics. They were **sectored** in ways that implied the underlying gene was being mutated and sometimes un-mutated repeatedly during kernel growth. She traced this to what she called **controlling elements**: chromosomal entities that could *move* from one location to another, disrupting a gene when they inserted into or near it, restoring function when they left.

She identified two components [McClintock 1950; McClintock 1953]:

- **Ds (Dissociation).** The movable element. When inserted into or near a colour gene, it disrupts expression. It was named "dissociation" because McClintock originally identified it by observing chromosome breakage at its location.
- **Ac (Activator).** A second element, elsewhere in the genome, whose presence was necessary for Ds to move. In modern terms, Ac encodes the transposase that acts on Ds.

The Ac/Ds system is now understood as a classical **DNA transposon family** with an autonomous element (Ac) carrying the transposase and non-autonomous elements (Ds, derived from Ac by internal deletions that destroyed the transposase coding sequence but preserved the transposase-recognised ends). Everything McClintock described from pure genetics — down to very specific timing-of-sector patterns — has a clean molecular explanation once you know the mechanism *(established)*.

## Reception

The canonical story is that McClintock's 1951 Cold Spring Harbor Symposium talk was received with bewildered silence and that she stopped publishing on the subject out of frustration. The canonical story is roughly right, with some nuance.

What's accurate:
- The formal response to her 1951 talk was muted. Several attendees later recalled not understanding her presentation.
- She published less on controlling elements after the mid-1950s than the scope of her findings would have warranted. She continued to research the topic through the 1960s but with reduced public visibility.
- Through the 1950s and 1960s, essentially no-one attempted to confirm or extend her specific findings outside her lab. Maize genetics of the kind she did was demanding, and her cytogenetic methods were idiosyncratic and hard to reproduce.

What's more complicated:
- She was already a major figure when she started the controlling-elements work. She had discovered transposition, crossing-over via chiasmata, and the nucleolus-organising region; she was one of the first women elected to the US National Academy of Sciences (1944) and was elected president of the Genetics Society of America (1945). The field did not *ignore* her — it ignored this particular line of work.
- Popular narratives sometimes overstate how completely she was dismissed. She retained institutional support, salary, and a lab at Cold Spring Harbor throughout. The neglect was intellectual, not professional.

Why was the work ignored? The standard answer, and I think basically correct: **maize genetics used a methodology no-one else could easily replicate**, and the mid-century consensus view of chromosome structure was static enough that "genes move around" had no available mechanism to explain it. The physicochemical tools to work out transposition at the molecular level didn't exist until the 1970s. When they did exist, they validated her *(my reading, widely shared)*.

## The rediscovery

Transposable elements were rediscovered in bacteria through the late 1960s:

- James Shapiro (1968), Peter Starlinger and colleagues (1968) identified insertion sequences (IS elements) in *E. coli*: DNA segments that inserted into genes, disrupting them, and occasionally jumped out.
- **Nancy Kleckner**'s work on Tn10 in *E. coli* in the 1970s directly characterised bacterial transposons.
- In yeast, fruit flies, and mammals, similar mobile elements were identified through the 1970s and 1980s.

Once the molecular picture emerged, McClintock's Ac/Ds could be read as what it was: the first identified transposon family, with all the mechanisms worked out before anyone had the vocabulary for them. She was awarded the 1983 **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine**, solo — an unusually direct acknowledgement.

## What transposons turn out to be

The shock, from a post-2000 perspective, is just how much of genomes is transposon-derived:

- **Human genome: ~45% transposon-derived.** Most of it is "dead" — ancient elements that have accumulated mutations and can no longer move. But some classes remain active: LINE-1 (autonomous retrotransposons), SINE (Alu elements, non-autonomous, ~1 million copies in humans), and HERV-K (a human endogenous retrovirus family).
- **Maize genome: ~85% transposon-derived** — the organism where it was first found has one of the highest fractions known.
- Active transposition in humans does occur in the germline and during early development, and at low rates in somatic tissues. LINE-1 activity in **neuronal progenitor cells** has been documented and may contribute to neuronal mosaicism [Muotri & Gage 2010; subsequent follow-ups].

Transposons have been implicated in:
- Genome evolution via co-option (exapted transposon sequences provide regulatory elements, exons, even whole genes — e.g. placental syncytin is an endogenous retrovirus env protein).
- Somatic mutation and disease (some cancers exhibit increased LINE-1 activity).
- Antibody diversification machinery (the V(D)J recombination RAG1/RAG2 enzymes are evolutionarily descended from an ancient transposase).

The old textbook line that most of the genome is "junk" has been substantially revised: much of it is transposon residue, and some of that residue is functional in ways we are still unpacking *(established as of the ENCODE-era consensus; specifics are active research)*.

## Disagreements and cautions

- **"McClintock was dismissed as a kook"** is exaggerated. She was respected; her specific line of work was neglected for ~15–20 years. The difference matters for lessons-drawing.
- **"Half the genome is transposons"** depends on how you measure. The 45% figure counts recognisable transposon-derived sequence. Broader estimates — including more ancient, more degraded elements detectable only by looser similarity — run to 60–70%. The figure is a lower bound.
- **Much of the transposon-derived sequence is *not* known to be functional.** The ENCODE project's controversial 2012 claim that "80% of the genome is functional" has been significantly tempered; most of the transposon residue is not under detectable selection. See Graur et al. 2013 critique.
- **Neuronal L1 activity** is real but its functional significance (does it matter for behaviour, disease?) is not settled.
- **Popular science occasionally conflates McClintock's controlling elements** with all subsequent transposon biology. Her specific findings (Ac/Ds in maize) are a small subset of what we now call transposable elements.

## Lessons I take from the case

Three things worth drawing out:

1. **Consensus can be wrong for decades.** The field's inability to make sense of McClintock's claims was not about her making errors; it was about the *absence of the tools* required to understand what she was saying. This pattern repeats elsewhere in science (Mendel, Semmelweis, Wegener).
2. **Methodological alien-ness delays recognition.** Part of why McClintock was hard to engage with was that her cytogenetic techniques and maize-specific insights were hard for non-specialists to evaluate. The lesson is not "invent easier methods" — sometimes the hard method is the right method — but "be alert to how much of peer evaluation is actually peer *fluency*."
3. **Senior scientists can ride it out.** McClintock kept her lab and did the work. She wasn't an outsider fighting institutional rejection; she was an insider being politely ignored on one line of her research. The story is instructive partly because she had the professional cushion to persist. Others would not have.

## Questions I'd like answered

1. **Is there a living McClintock right now** — someone with a 20–30 year head start on the field, whose work is neglected because the interpretive framework isn't ready? If yes, how would we identify them?
2. **What fraction of the human genome's transposon-derived sequence is actually under selection** vs just neutral fossil? The ENCODE / Graur debate is still unresolved.
3. **Does somatic L1 activity in the brain matter for cognition or psychiatric disease**? Promising and unproven.
4. **Are there still undiscovered classes of mobile elements** in poorly-sequenced eukaryotes? Some recent work on protists suggests yes.
5. **When McClintock saw the 1983 Nobel citation**, what did she think? The public record is quiet; the private accounts are moving.
