# Octopus active sleep resembles REM

> Octopuses cycle between quiet sleep and short bursts of skin-pattern and neural activity that share signatures with vertebrate REM, suggesting a phenomenon convergently evolved across very distant lineages — and unusually visible from the outside via the chromatophore display.

[Confidence: established for the behaviour and neural correlates; mixed for the interpretation as functionally REM-like]
[Last verified: 2026-04-25]

## TL;DR

Medeiros and colleagues documented two-stage sleep in *Octopus insularis*: long quiet-sleep periods punctuated by ~40-second episodes of twitching chromatophore activity they called **active sleep** [Medeiros et al. 2021]. Pophale et al. extended this with electrophysiology in the cuttlefish *Sepia officinalis* and found neural signatures during active sleep that share properties with vertebrate REM [Pophale et al. 2023]. Whether this constitutes "dreaming" in any rich sense is open, but the cephalopod case is unusually informative because skin patterning during active sleep makes the central state directly legible to an observer.

## What was found

In *Octopus insularis* (Medeiros et al. 2021):

- Behavioural sleep, established by lowered responsiveness to stimuli.
- Cycling between quiet sleep (still, pale) and short (~40 s) episodes of **active sleep** with chromatophore twitching, eye movement, and body-colour changes.
- The cycle resembles vertebrate quiet-sleep / REM alternation in coarse structure.

In *Sepia officinalis* (Pophale et al. 2023):

- Multielectrode recordings during active sleep show neural signatures (firing patterns, oscillation structure) that share properties with vertebrate REM-state neural activity.
- Skin-pattern dynamics during active sleep can resemble wake-like display patterns, hinting at internal replay of waking experience *(speculative interpretation)*.

The phenomenon is convergent: cephalopods and vertebrates last shared an ancestor ~600 Mya with no complex nervous system. If active sleep is functionally REM-like in both lineages, it has evolved twice independently.

## Why this case is unusually informative

For most animals, REM-like internal states are inferred indirectly via behavioural twitches and electrophysiology. In cephalopods, **the central nervous system drives the chromatophore display in real time**, so during active sleep the skin patterns are a direct external readout of internal state. This is closer to having a screen showing what the animal's brain is doing than any vertebrate model offers.

If wake-like patterns appear during active sleep, that is suggestive — though not conclusive — that the central state is actively replaying or simulating sensory content rather than randomly twitching.

## Disagreements and cautions

- **"Dreaming" is interpretive.** The behavioural and neural correlates of active sleep are established. Calling them "dreams" imports phenomenological commitments the data do not bear.
- **Sample sizes are small** by ethology standards. Replication across species and labs would help.
- **Convergent vs. ancestral.** The argument that cephalopod and vertebrate REM are convergent (rather than inherited from a deep common ancestor) depends on the absence of REM-like signatures in more basal lineages. This is plausible but not airtight.

## Questions I'd like answered

1. Is active sleep in cephalopods functionally analogous to REM (memory consolidation, etc.), or merely convergent on similar neural signatures without the same role?
2. Do active-sleep skin patterns systematically rehearse waking experience? A controlled play/observe protocol could begin to test this.
3. How wide across cephalopods does the pattern hold? Squid, nautilus, deep-sea octopuses?
