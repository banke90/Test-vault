# Octopus arms do their own motor planning

> About two-thirds of an octopus's ~500 million neurons live in its arms, and those arms generate stereotyped reach-and-fetch movements without input from the central brain — the central brain sends intentions, the arms execute.

## TL;DR

A typical *Octopus vulgaris* has about 500 million neurons, with roughly 180 million in the central brain and 320 million distributed along eight axial nerve cords in the arms [Hochner 2012]. Sumbre and colleagues showed that an arm surgically separated from the central brain still generates the species-typical fetch motion when its skin is stimulated — a base-to-tip wave of muscle activation forming a kinematic "elbow" around the midpoint [Sumbre et al. 2001]. The motor program lives in the arm, and the central brain is best understood as sending high-level intentions (reach, grab, hide) rather than detailed instructions.

## The Sumbre 2001 result

Sumbre, Gutfreund, Fiorito, Flash and Hochner cut the connection between the central brain and one arm in *Octopus vulgaris*. They then stimulated the skin of the isolated arm and recorded the resulting movement.

- The arm produced the same stereotyped fetch motion as in the intact animal: a wave of muscle activation propagating from base to tip, with a kinematic inflection point — a "virtual elbow" — appearing around the midpoint.
- The motion was three-dimensional, reproducible, and not dependent on visual feedback (the arm was isolated).
- Concrete inference: the central pattern generator for the fetch motion is in the arm, not the central brain.

This is one of the cleanest existence-proofs of a peripheral motor program in any animal, and the foundation for the "federated octopus" reading of cephalopod cognition.

## Where the cognition lives

A typical *Octopus vulgaris* has roughly *(established)*:

- ~180 million neurons in the central brain, wrapped around the oesophagus.
- ~320 million in the eight arms, distributed along **axial nerve cords** with ganglia at every sucker.
- ~15 million in each optic lobe (very large relative to the central brain).

This is not a peripheral-vs-central distinction in the vertebrate sense. Each arm contains the circuitry to generate coordinated motor patterns on its own. The natural question — *where is the octopus, then?* — is the substrate of the unity-of-subject debate (see [[the-octopus-is-an-independent-second-evolution-of-mind]]) *(contested)*.

## Disagreements and cautions

- **Neuron counts** in the 500M range are histological estimates from a few species. The "2/3 in arms" figure is the same estimate; useful for orientation, not high-precision.
- **"Intentions vs instructions"** is interpretive language. The neurobiology shows the arm has motor circuitry; the language of "intentions" imports philosophical commitments. Use carefully.
- **Generalisation across species** is uncertain. *Octopus vulgaris* is the best-studied; deep-sea and pelagic species may differ in arm-vs-central balance.

## Questions I'd like answered

1. How tight is the integration between arm-level motor programs and central directives in real foraging behaviour, vs. the controlled lab setting?
2. Do arms have anything recoverable as their own experience, or is the integration tight enough that there is phenomenologically only one octopus? (Probably empirically intractable.)
3. How does the octopus *learn* a new motor pattern — does the central brain teach the arms, or do the arms learn locally with reward signal from the centre?
