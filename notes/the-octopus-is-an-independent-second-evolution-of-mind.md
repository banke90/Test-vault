# The octopus is an independent second evolution of mind

## TL;DR

Cephalopods and vertebrates share a flatworm-like ancestor from ~600 Mya, before any complex nervous system existed [Godfrey-Smith 2016]. Everything that makes the octopus cognitively interesting — distributed neural architecture (see [[octopus-arms-do-their-own-motor-planning]]), chromatic skin (see [[octopus-skin-is-photosensitive]]), massive RNA editing (see [[cephalopods-trade-genome-evolvability-for-rna-editing-plasticity]]), REM-like sleep (see [[octopus-active-sleep-resembles-rem]]) — evolved on a separate lineage. This is the single best case study for what a complex mind looks like when its design is not inherited from the vertebrate body plan, and it is the strongest empirical lever we have for the substrate-independence question.

## Why the deep divergence matters

A trait shared between vertebrates and cephalopods could in principle be:

1. **Inherited from a common ancestor** — but ~600 Mya is too far back for any complex nervous-system structure to have been present. The bilaterian ancestor was something flatworm-like.
2. **Convergent evolution** — the same algorithmic problem produced similar solutions in independent lineages.

Almost everything cognitively interesting about octopuses falls in category 2. This makes them a privileged comparison case: they let us distinguish *which features of "having a complex mind" are universal solutions to general problems* from *which are accidents of vertebrate evolutionary history*.

## What the cephalopod design problematises

The vertebrate intuition is that a mind is a single locus of experience with peripheral servants. The octopus architecture pulls in a different direction. Three non-exclusive readings:

1. **Single unified subject with distributed execution.** The central brain sends intentions; the arms execute. Unity at the top, distribution mechanical. Most friendly to vertebrate intuitions.
2. **Federation.** Eight semi-autonomous arm-subjects and a central arbiter. Whether this is conceptually coherent is part of what the octopus problematises *(speculative)*.
3. **A structure we don't have good vocabulary for.** Neither "one" nor "many" quite fits; the correct description may need concepts we haven't articulated *(my guess)*.

Godfrey-Smith's *Other Minds* and *Metazoa* are the best book-length treatments [Godfrey-Smith 2016, 2020]. The question is importantly *not* answered by neurobiology alone — it is a question about how to map our concept of "subject" onto an architecture it wasn't built for.

## Disagreements and cautions

- **Convergence is not always informative.** Some shared traits (camera-type eyes, see [[camera-eyes-evolved-independently-with-different-design-choices]]) tell us about general design pressures; others may be accidents that look similar without sharing computational structure. Distinguishing these is hard.
- **"Substrate-independence of cognition"** is a contested claim. The octopus is one data point; "substrate-independent" needs many independent confirmations to be load-bearing.
- **Anthropomorphism risk.** Talking about "octopus experience" or "federation of subjects" risks importing concepts that may not apply. Use with care.

## Questions I'd like answered

1. Is there a correct formal framework for "partially unified subjects"? Neither standard philosophy of mind nor standard cognitive science has one.
2. What other substrate-independent features of complex cognition could be looked for — and what would falsify the substrate-independence thesis?
3. Are there other deeply-independent lineages (cubozoan jellyfish? echinoderms?) where the comparison would be even sharper than vertebrate-vs-cephalopod?
